## The short version

**The FCC's public record of who owns local TV stations understates how concentrated local television is in practice. Dozens of stations are run by a different company than the one that holds the license, through agreements the ownership record doesn't show. Counting them, 53 of the 210 U.S. TV markets are more concentrated in operation than on paper.**

A TV station's license can sit with one company while another sells its advertising, runs its day-to-day operations, and sometimes supplies its programming. These arrangements are called local marketing, joint sales and shared services agreements (LMAs, JSAs and SSAs), and the company holding the license is often called a "sidecar." The FCC counted joint sales agreements toward its ownership limits for a few years, then stopped in 2017. I wanted to know how much the public record misses as a result, so I measured it in every market in the country. If the industry is new to you, the primer below covers the terms.

How big the gap is depends on what you count as control, so the numbers come in two versions: one counting every documented agreement, and a narrower one counting only agreements under which the operator also programs the station.

<Stats />

**What the work involved:** building the ownership of all 1,347 local commercial stations covered here from the FCC's own filings; tracing 63 operating agreements, station by station, to the operators' reports to the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) or to the FCC's own records; checking the automated pipeline against four markets I'd built by hand; and following four markets back to 2009 to date when things changed.

## A short primer on local TV ownership

If you don't follow the broadcast industry, these are the pieces you need for the rest of the page.

**Stations and licenses.** A local TV station broadcasts over the public airwaves, so it needs a license from the Federal Communications Commission (FCC). The company holding the license, the *licensee*, is legally responsible for the station. Stations go by their call letters, like WBFF in Baltimore.

**Markets.** Nielsen, the ratings company, divides the country into 210 TV markets. Each is a group of counties whose viewers mostly watch the same local stations. Chicago is one; so is Victoria, Texas. The FCC's ownership rules use these markets.

**Which stations count here.** This page counts *commercial full-power stations*: the ordinary local channels that sell advertising and are licensed at full power. In this data, 57% are ABC, CBS, NBC or Fox affiliates. The rest carry networks like CW, Univision and Telemundo, or run independent, religious or shopping programming. Three kinds of station are left out. Noncommercial stations, mostly PBS member stations, don't run commercials, and the ownership limits exempt them. Low-power and Class A stations have smaller signals that reach only part of a market, and the limits don't count them. Stations that are off the air are left out too. That leaves 1,347. From here on, "stations" means these.

**Networks and station groups.** The networks supply much of what airs, but they own fewer than one in ten of these stations. Most are owned by station groups, companies with stations in many markets. The three biggest in this data are Gray (182 stations), Nexstar (166) and Sinclair (139).

**Ownership limits.** The FCC limits how many stations one company may "own, operate, or control" in a single market: two at most, and only under conditions. The limit is written in stations, which is why this study counts stations rather than revenue or viewers.

**Attribution.** Whether a tie to a station counts as ownership under those limits is set by the FCC's attribution rules. A tie that counts is *attributable*. That's the word to watch: an agreement that isn't attributable lets a company run a station without it counting against the limit.

**The three agreements.**

- **LMA (local marketing agreement, also called time brokerage).** The operator buys blocks of the station's airtime, fills them with its own programming, and sells the ads in them. If the operator already has a station in the same market and brokers more than 15% of the other station's weekly airtime, the FCC's rules count the brokered station as the operator's.
- **JSA (joint sales agreement).** The operator sells the station's advertising. The FCC made these count toward the limits in 2014 and reversed itself in 2017. Today they don't count.
- **SSA (shared services agreement).** The stations share staff, equipment or services. The FCC's rule lists "administrative, technical, sales, and/or programming support." These have never counted toward the limits.

Under an LMA or a JSA, the licensee has to certify in writing that it keeps "ultimate control" over the station's finances, personnel and programming. Stations also have to post copies of all three kinds of agreement in the online public file the FCC hosts for each station. But those copies sit in each station's own file. They aren't connected to the ownership record, which still lists each station under its licensee.

**Why it matters to track them.**

1. **The limits.** If an agreement doesn't count, one company can run more of a market's stations than it could legally own. That's what the FCC itself called "a loophole" in 2014.
2. **Advertisers.** Under a JSA, two stations that look like rivals have one company selling their ads.
3. **Local voices.** When it closed the JSA loophole in 2014, the FCC said the change would help ensure "competition, localism, and diversity in local broadcast markets." When one company runs several stations, a market has fewer independent decision-makers than its station count suggests. Whether that changes what's on the air is a separate and contested question, and this study doesn't measure content.
4. **Measuring at all.** Merger reviewers, antitrust enforcers, reporters and researchers work from the ownership record. If it shows six owners where two companies run the stations, every analysis built on it starts out wrong.

## 1. Measuring the gap

**63 stations are run under documented agreements.** Nexstar runs 37, Sinclair 16 and Gray 6. Three regional companies run the other four: Morgan Murphy Media (two), Bahakel Communications and Coastal Television. Every one is cited to the operator's own SEC filing or to an FCC filing. The license stays with a separate company in every case, and these licensees file their ownership reports as independent companies, so in the ownership record the stations look independent.

To measure concentration I used the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), the standard measure in antitrust. Take each company's share of a market's stations, square it, add them up, and scale so the maximum is 10,000, which means one company runs everything. Six stations with six separate owners score 1,667. The federal merger guidelines, the Justice Department and Federal Trade Commission's rules of thumb for reviewing mergers, call a market "highly concentrated" above 1,800. I scored each market twice: once grouping stations by the owners the FCC's filings disclose, with parent companies rolled up (**the record**), and once assigning each station run under an agreement to the company that runs it (**operation**).

**What it doesn't show.** The shares are counts of stations, not revenue or audience. That matches the FCC's local limit, so it measures the thing the rule regulates. It isn't a measure of power in the advertising market, which is what a merger reviewer would look at. It says nothing about what's on the air.

<AboutData>

- **Sources:** FCC ownership filings from its Licensing and Management System, a snapshot dated October 1, 2026; the operators' annual and current reports to the SEC (10-Ks and 8-Ks); and the agreements listed in the FCC's ownership filings.
- **Two definitions of control:** the broad one gives a station to its operator under any documented LMA, JSA or SSA. The narrow one counts only agreements under which the operator also programs the station. The operators' filings say the licensee keeps programming under JSAs and SSAs, so whether those amount to control is a judgment call. The two definitions bracket it. Nine agreements, including Gray's six, don't say who programs the station, so they count only under the broad definition.
- **Why not revenue:** station-level revenue is proprietary. Weighting by it could push the numbers either way: in my July run, stations under agreements were about as likely to carry ABC, CBS, NBC or Fox as other stations, and an operator pairing one strong station with two weak ones would score *lower* on revenue than on station counts.
- **Which agreements count:** only ones documented in the operators' SEC filings or in FCC filings. An earlier version of the list had seven entries those filings don't support, four of them resting on a single Wikipedia citation, and they're out. Removing them erased Charleston, South Carolina's whole effect and took Birmingham back under 1,800.
- **How far to trust it:** under the broad definition every figure is a floor, because only agreements I could document are counted. The narrow definition is the conservative reading.

</AboutData>

## 2. Baltimore consolidated in 2013, and the record never showed it

<PanelChart />

Baltimore has six stations. From 2009 through 2023 the FCC record showed six separate owners in every filing year, an HHI of 1,667 that never moved. In operation it looked different from the start. Sinclair owned WBFF and also ran WNUV, whose license sat with a sidecar called Cunningham. In 2013 Fox sold WUTB to another sidecar, Deerfield Media, and Sinclair began running that station too, under a joint sales agreement. Sinclair now ran half the market's stations, and operational HHI rose from 2,222 to 3,333. Someone reading only the FCC record for those fourteen years would conclude Baltimore's TV market never consolidated.

Sinclair bought WUTB outright in October 2025, so the record now shows part of it: 2,222 against 3,333 in operation.

**What it doesn't show.** This history covers four markets, three of them picked because they contain agreements. It can date events, but it can't say how common they are nationally or whether the practice is growing. Extending it to all 210 markets is the biggest piece of work left.

<AboutData>

- **Sources:** the ownership reports stations file with the FCC every two years, 2009–2023; Sinclair's 2025 annual report to the SEC for the WUTB agreement and the 2025 purchase; for Wichita Falls, the agreements in KAUZ's online public file.
- **The other three markets I followed:** in Amarillo, operational concentration rose 408 points in 2019, when Gray bought two stations. That one showed up in the record too, because Gray bought them outright. Wichita Falls never moved. Its four stations have four owners on the record (2,500), but two companies ran all four in every year from 2009 (5,000): Nexstar with Mission's KJTL, and KSWO's operator with KAUZ. That second operator changed twice, from KSWO's owner to Raycom to Gray; the concentration didn't. KAUZ's agreements are dated 2009 to 2018, and that they stayed in force between those dates is an inference. Chicago has 14 stations and no documented agreements, so it can't show what an agreement does. I built it by hand to check that the code doesn't give a station to an operator it shouldn't.

- **How far to trust it:** I built all four markets by hand before writing the national pipeline, and the pipeline reproduces them exactly.

## 3. All 210 markets

<MarketsChart />

Counting every documented agreement, **53 of the 210 markets** are more concentrated in operation than on the record, by an average of **1,215 points**. In **Lake Charles and Victoria**, both two-station markets, one company runs every station. The number of markets where one company runs at least half the stations rises from **56 to 77**, and the TV households in them from **7.5 million to 13.3 million**. Three markets, **Providence, Dayton and Jackson, Mississippi** (1.4 million TV households between them), sit at or below the 1,800 line on the record and above it in operation.

The narrow definition shrinks all of this. Counting only agreements under which the operator also programs the station, **12 markets** move, majority-run markets go from 56 to **62** (7.5 million to 9.9 million households), only Victoria is run entirely by one company, and only Providence crosses 1,800. The agreements behind those 12 are all LMAs, which the FCC's rules count as ownership once they cover more than 15% of a station's weekly airtime. Even so, the ownership record lists those stations under their licensees. And 41 of the 53 markets move only because of agreements that leave programming with the licensee, or that don't say who programs. The switch above the chart shows both.

**What it doesn't show.** The 1,800 line is a benchmark, not a prediction. Merger reviewers apply it to advertising revenue, not station counts, so crossing it here doesn't mean a deal would be blocked. And this is a snapshot of October 2026. The only evidence on trends is the four-market history above.

<AboutData>

- **Sources:** the FCC snapshot of October 1, 2026. TV households are the FCC's 2015 figures, the only ones its data carries.
- **How much data:** 1,347 stations and 113.8 million TV households. The 53 markets that move hold 19.7 million households, 17% of the total.
- **Pending sales:** the FCC's database lists the buyer as licensee as soon as a sale is filed. On October 1, 2026, 12 stations had a sale pending, and for those the record here uses the licensee in the station's latest ownership report. Otherwise it would credit deals the FCC hasn't approved: Providence would already be over the line on the record (2,222) and drop out of the markets that cross it.
- **How the majority count works:** the 56 markets on the record include 10 that have only one station, which count automatically. Leave them out and the change is the same: 46 to 67 (broad) or 46 to 52 (programming only).
- **How far to trust it:** this counts every market and station rather than a sample, so there's no sampling error on the counts. The uncertainty is in the agreements. If all 12 unconfirmed leads I've found check out, 61 markets move and 6 cross the line. If one operator's agreements were all wrong, the result would shrink but not disappear: drop every Nexstar agreement and 25 markets still move; drop Sinclair's and 40 do.

</AboutData>

## 4. Small markets feel it more, but they aren't targeted more

My first chart of this made it look like a small-market problem. The average gap was largest in two- and three-station markets and shrank with size, and I wrote that sidecars cluster where ownership limits bind hardest. A test didn't support it, and I withdrew it.

Two things were mixed together. The size of the effect does fall with market size, but mostly as arithmetic: handing one station of three to a rival moves the index far more than one station of twenty, whatever anyone intended. And sidecar stations do look more common in small markets. Markets with nine or more stations hold 14 of them, where scattering them at random would put about 26, a shortfall chance would produce about once in a thousand tries. But Nexstar, Sinclair and Gray, the companies that run nearly all of them, own 53% of the stations in two- and three-station markets and 21% in markets with 13 or more. Count only their stations, and placement doesn't depend on market size: 14 where chance would put about 16, a gap random placement produces about a third of the time.

What's left is a point about consequences, not intent. The same agreement that barely registers in a twenty-station market turns Victoria into a one-company market. And because three of the operators are regional companies working in small markets like Victoria, Joplin and Meridian, a disclosure rule that only covered the national groups would miss them, and in a market that small a single missed agreement is the difference between two operators and one.

## 5. Why the record can't see it

In 2014 the FCC made television joint sales agreements count toward its ownership limits. Announcing the rule, it said it was closing "a loophole" that had "resulted in consolidation in excess of what is permitted" under its rules. In 2017 it repealed the joint sales rule, and in 2021 the Supreme Court upheld the repeal in *FCC v. Prometheus Radio Project*. Shared services agreements have never counted. Much of the concentration measured here is what the agency once wrote a rule to count, and then stopped counting.

The FCC does keep these agreements, in two places, and neither tells you from the ownership record which station is run by whom. Stations post copies in their online public files, one station at a time. And ownership filings have to list contracts that bear on control: the FCC's database holds thousands of LMA, JSA and SSA entries. But those entries attach to filings, not stations. Of the filings that list a control agreement, 45% cover more than one station (one covers 869), and the entry names companies, not the station being run or which side runs it. So the FCC's own data can't say which stations are under an agreement. Two of the sidecar companies on my list, Vaughan and American Spirit, don't appear in those entries at all. That's why the list here is built from the operators' SEC filings, with the FCC's entries used only for the four stations run by private regional companies.

The FCC's data shows one more trace of the repeal. Disclosures of joint sales agreements rose about ninefold when the 2014 rule took effect (from 1.7 to 16.0 per 1,000 filings), then fell by more than half over the three cycles after the repeal (to 7.7). I'd treat that as suggestive only. The total number of filings also dropped sharply after 2015, so some of the change may reflect who was filing.

The arrangements also turn into ownership when that suits the operator. Gray agreed in July 2026 to buy all six of its sidecar's stations. Sinclair filed in August and September 2026 to take the licenses of four Cunningham stations, and in 2025 it bought four Deerfield stations, Baltimore's WUTB among them. As those deals close, the concentration appears in the record. My reading is that sidecars work as placeholders: the record catches up when the arrangement ends, not while it runs.

## 6. What I'd ask for

One thing: **make the ownership record show who runs the stations.** Above a size threshold, LMAs, JSAs and SSAs should be reported in the ownership record against the stations they cover, naming the company that runs each one. Stations already post copies of these agreements in their public files, so the information exists. It just isn't where anyone measuring ownership looks.

I'd ask for this rather than tighter ownership limits because it's content-neutral, it doesn't require proving harm, and it doesn't bring back any caps. When the FCC required stations to disclose shared services agreements in 2016, it called disclosure "a necessary step" before considering whether to count them. Whether these agreements should count toward the ownership limits is a further choice, one the FCC made for JSAs in 2014 and reversed in 2017. My reading of *Prometheus* is that it settled whether the FCC could loosen its ownership rules on a thin record, not what the ownership record has to show, so a rule about what the record must show raises a different legal question. That's an argument, not something the Court said.

Who can act, starting with those who can move soonest:

1. **Antitrust enforcers** at the Justice Department, the FTC and state attorneys general can look at local TV markets at their operational concentration now, when they review mergers or investigate business practices, without waiting for the FCC.
2. **Congress** can write the requirement into law. That's the version that would hold even if a future FCC wanted fewer rules.
3. **The FCC** reviews its ownership rules every four years and takes public comments. This analysis can go in as one, asking it to bring the agreements stations already file into the ownership record and to make JSAs count again. Even if the Commission doesn't act, a comment puts the numbers on the public record.
4. **Reporters and researchers** can check any market against public filings, and every agreement they can document raises the floor.

## How it was checked

- **Owners.** The pipeline groups stations into owners by following who appears on whose ownership filings. A stray name in one filing, such as a job title typed where a company name belongs, can fuse unrelated station groups without breaking any of the arithmetic. A script lists every name that on its own holds otherwise unconnected stations together, and each one is checked against outside sources.
- **Hand-built markets.** Four markets were built by hand before the national pipeline existed, and the pipeline reproduces them exactly. That confirms the pipeline groups stations into owners the way careful hand work does. It doesn't confirm the agreements, because both draw on the same evidence.
- **Agreements.** Each one is checked against the operator's own SEC filings and, for the biggest sidecar companies, against the agreements listed in FCC ownership filings, which name them hundreds of times.
- **Automated checks.** 27 checks guard the pipeline, and a script checks the headline figures in the written drafts against its outputs.

## Where it stands

This is a working draft. It hasn't been peer reviewed, and the October revision hasn't had an independent read yet. The next steps are extending the 2009–2023 history to all 210 markets and confirming the remaining leads. It's the broadcast piece of a larger project on media consolidation, chosen because local TV is the one corner of the media economy where the government's own records make this comparison possible.
