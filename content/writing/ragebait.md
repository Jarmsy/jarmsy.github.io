I was passively watching a stream when the streamer pulled up a video someone in chat had shared with them. It was seemingly made to mislead the audience, and it was inflammatory as it was about a polarizing cultural issue. Whether it was AI generated or not, it was presented as real, and the actor behind it was presumably not genuine.

It also wasn't the only video like it on that topic, and it had a decent amount of engagement. That's what got me thinking about how much of our attention and perspective content like this can capture, and who is to blame for it.

Online, this kind of content is called ragebait: inflammatory, often exaggerated, sometimes fake, and made to get a reaction. A month after I first wrote this, Oxford University Press named "rage bait" its [2025 Word of the Year](https://www.npr.org/2025/12/01/nx-s1-5627179/rage-bait-oxford-word-of-the-year), defining it as online content "deliberately designed to elicit anger or outrage," usually posted to drive engagement. I'm borrowing the word because I saw a parallel to something that happens in person too, where a disingenuous actor riles someone up for their own benefit or enjoyment.

Media has always shaped how people think. Newspapers and cable news defined worldviews for generations. What's different now is how it's delivered. It's served by algorithms, tuned for emotional response, and on all day.

Ragebait is the sharpest version of this. It doesn't care what you believe, only that you feel it strongly. Strong feelings make us more likely to share and react, and less likely to pause.

I see this in myself. I tend to engage more with content that hits me emotionally, whether positively or negatively, though which one it is changes the energy I bring to it.

The reactions to that video split into the two most common ways I've seen people engage with ragebait:

1. **Agreeing changes what we believe.** The content confirms something we already suspected, so we don't check it. We feel righteous, maybe share it as proof, and a claim nobody verified becomes part of how we see the world. Engaging with it also tells the algorithm to show us more of the same, and over time that becomes an echo chamber: the content and the people around us mostly agree, and opposing views rarely get through. Agreement starts to feel like consensus. It gets harder to notice when we're wrong, and our views harden without anything pushing back on them.
2. **Disagreeing changes how we see and treat other people.** Pushing back feels like the responsible thing to do, but every reply and stitch carries the video to people who wouldn't have seen it otherwise. The person in the video can't see us back, and everyone else in the chat or comments is faceless, so we hold back less than we would face to face. It's why some of the worst impulses show up in comments and DMs. The people who fell for it also start to stand in for everyone on the other side, and that side starts to look worse than it is. We come away more pessimistic about each other.

Both leave us less empathetic and less open-minded toward people who only seem to disagree with us, and that doesn't stay online. It carries into how we treat people in real life.

Either way it counts as engagement, and the algorithm serves up more.

This isn't always accidental. Centralized actors (media corporations, political commentators, institutions) and decentralized ones (individual creators, influencers, bots) both benefit from our division. Ragebait often isn't sincere.

The people producing it cherry-pick anecdotes, strip claims of their context, and pass off AI deepfakes as real footage. Some know full well that it deepens ideological trenches. Others have convinced themselves it's a decent way to make a living. They don't need to lie outright. They only need us to feel something strong enough that we stop thinking clearly.

Ask who is most responsible and things get foggy. People who share it say they're just passing along what they saw. Creators say it's what gets views and blame the platform. Platforms point to the individual's algorithm. Executives cite fiduciary duty. Everyone ends up at "human nature," or at blaming consumers for creating the demand.

"Blame human nature" is a thought-terminating cliché, and it shows up well beyond media. I see it as a post-hoc rationalization that talks us out of imagining a better situation. I think critical thought and empathy, and the will to keep practicing both, are what break these cycles, personally and systemically.

I don't put the blame for inflammatory content on individuals. That doesn't mean individuals are powerless.

In high school AP U.S. History, I was trained to break down historical documents: Who wrote this? What was the context? Who was the audience? What was the purpose?

What if we asked the same questions of TikToks, tweets, YouTube clips, or the headlines we see every day?

- Who created this?
- What do they want me to feel or think?
- What could be missing? Alternative perspectives, testimonies, facts?
- What questions didn't they ask?

We don't need to disengage from the world to think clearly. We need to pause before reacting, and to notice when a single piece of content is changing what we think more than it should ([check out Bayesian Thinking](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UrO__x4R-_M&t=64s)). It also helps to want truth more than we want justice or outrage.

Ragebait isn't going away, but those four questions cost almost nothing to ask. If they come before the share button, that's one fewer person spreading it.
