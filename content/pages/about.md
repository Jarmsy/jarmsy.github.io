I'm John. I trained as a mechanical engineer, I fly small planes, and I've just moved to Hyde Park in Chicago to study computational analysis and public policy at the University of Chicago's Harris School. This page is the long version of why those three things belong together.

## My layers of interest

A year ago I described my worldview as "an often reformulating understanding of the structures of life that begin with quantum physics, go through biology, to neuroscience, psychology, and the behavior of humans past and present." I stand by that, with one floor added on top: institutions, and the policies that come out of them.

The through-line is that I want to understand how beliefs, decisions and policies emerge from the systems that produce them. Systems are made of people, so understanding people is most of the work.

## Then and now

In 2025 I journaled that I was looking for an outlet to apply myself at some intersection of engineering and politics, where I could do work that advances things in a good direction. I didn't have a name for it yet.

What gave it a name was noticing the same pattern in two very different places.

On the shop floor — first as a production operator at 3M, later as an engineer designing fixtures, tools and machine guarding at Sub-Zero and 3M — I learned that whether a safety system actually works depends on whether it accounts for operator fatigue, attention, time pressure and judgment. To design a tool that fits an existing process, you have to step into the world of the person who'll use it under real operational constraints.

In the cockpit, the same lesson. Flight training is built on structured risk management, redundancy and procedural discipline — and yet a striking number of engine failures come from pilots mismanaging the fuel system: forgetting to switch tanks, misplanning fuel for a trip. The engine was fine, but it failed due to a silly human error. This got me thinking about safety and how to design systems, with the understanding that we must minimize the opportunities for humans to make errors while maximizing the reliability of automatic systems. That led me to read how the FAA approaches pilot mental health, and to notice how a cautious regulatory posture shapes what data can even exist: disclosure carries professional risk in the profession, so the evidence is self-reported, indirect, or missing.

Across both: human behavior is shaped by the institutional environments people operate within, and it is shaped and responds to the incentives those environments create. I wanted the tools to study that rigorously. That's a large part why I chose the MSCAPP program and initiated a shift in my career goals.

## Working with people

Most of what I've gotten done on a team came from finding out what was actually in someone's way.

At Sub-Zero, the fabrication of my fixture parts was taking longer than I'd hoped. So I went into the shop to meet the one machinist in charge of every custom component that came off the lathes and CNC machines. I got to know him and how much work he was carrying, we chopped it up about family, the Packers, and our shared interest in new manufacturing technology. Mysteriously, my parts were done within a few days. I immediately built my fixture and was proud to go back to visit and show him how it all came together.

On my [senior design project](/made/tpms-electrolysis/) I set the direction week by week, which mostly meant staying late with teammates to work through concepts nobody was sure of yet, and connecting the less glamorous tasks — the literature review, say — to the result we were all after. That's how we found the [paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-95399-8) our whole approach ended up building on.

At 3M, I led the intern innovation project: a group of interns from different specialties, put together to write one proposal. Everyone brought their perspectives for how to approach improving the product from angles unique to their expertise. I initiated meetings with the engineers of the product and mediated the conversation to facilitate the deployment of each team member's strength. In the end, we gave a proposal that combined our ideas on a machine-vision quality-control system for EBOI fiber-optic connectors, which the data-center boom has since made one of 3M's most sought-after products.

## Flying

I've always tested and trained my coordination on something — sports, video games, driving. Flight training started as one more skill that I've always wanted to build and became a private pilot license (2024) and an instrument rating (2025). It's also where I learned to value taking checklists seriously.

Flying is such an incredible privilege. It has allowed me to get a new perspective both of the Midwest from the air, and inside our National Airspace System. Which I see as a model of an excellently built system that facilitates travel so efficiently and safely.

## Along the way

Before any of this I fueled and towed jets on the ramp at a Madison airport, cooked and served at a bar, and ran web-handling equipment on a manufacturing line. I list those on purpose. They're where I learned what salt-of-the-earth work looks like.

## What I'm after

I want to be a positive force in the change I'd like to see — and to grow into someone who creates more: good times with the people I love, writing that others connect with, and policy and tools that make things better for people.
