I'm John. I trained as a mechanical engineer, I fly small planes, and I've just moved to Hyde Park in Chicago to study computational analysis and public policy at the University of Chicago's Harris School. This page is the long version of why those three things belong together.

## The stack

A year ago I described my worldview as "an often reformulating understanding of the structures of life that begin with quantum physics, go through biology, to neuroscience, psychology, and the behavior of humans past and present." I stand by that, with one floor added on top: institutions, and the policies that come out of them.

The through-line is that I want to understand how beliefs, decisions and policies emerge from the systems that produce them. Systems are made of people, so understanding people is most of the work.

## Then and now

In 2025 I wrote that I was "actively soul searching" for "some intersection of engineering and politics" where I could do work that advances things in a good direction. I didn't have a name for it yet.

What gave it a name was noticing the same pattern in two very different places.

On the shop floor — first as a production operator at 3M, later as an engineer designing fixtures, tools and machine guarding at Sub-Zero — I learned that whether a safety system actually works depends on whether it accounts for operator fatigue, attention, time pressure and judgment. To design a tool that fits an existing process, you have to step into the world of the person who'll use it under real constraints. Safety turned out to be a property that emerges from how people interact with a system, not a rule you impose afterwards.

In the cockpit, the same lesson. Flight training is built on structured risk management, redundancy and procedural discipline — and yet a striking number of engine failures come from pilots mismanaging the fuel system: forgetting to switch tanks, misplanning fuel for a trip. Cognitive failures, not mechanical ones. That led me to read how the FAA approaches pilot mental health, and to notice how a cautious regulatory posture shapes what data can even exist: disclosure carries professional risk, so the evidence is self-reported, indirect, or missing.

Across both: human behavior is shaped by the institutional environments people operate within, and it responds — sometimes predictably, sometimes stubbornly — to the incentives those environments create. I wanted the tools to study that rigorously. That's what the MSCAPP program is for.

## Flying

I've always tested my coordination on something — sports, video games, driving. Flight training started as one more skill to build and became a private pilot licence (2024) and an instrument rating (2025). It's also where I learned to take checklists seriously, which explains more about this website than it should.

## Along the way

Before any of this I fueled and towed jets on the ramp at a Madison airport, cooked and served at a bar, and ran web-handling equipment on a manufacturing line. I list those on purpose. They're where I learned what work looks like from the inside of a system rather than from the drawing.

## What I'm after

I'm not chasing recognition. I want to be a positive force in the change I'd like to see — and to grow into someone who creates more: good times with the people I love, writing that others connect with, and policy and tools that make things better for people.
